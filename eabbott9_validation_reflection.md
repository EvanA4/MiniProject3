# Validation Reflection

### Adjucated Label Patterns

More often than not, I (the validator) was being too strict when determining whether a project recovered after its largest gap. Whenever I disagreed with my partner, it was always me who felt the project hadn't truly recovered. I would say it was still a 50/50 whether the adjucated value was mine or my partner's.

### Signals Preceding Long Gaps

Since many projects had major feature development commits just before their largest gap, my partner and I figured that feature commits were a good indicator for gaps. Another common pattern for projects was the volume of commits before a gap. Usually, if there was a large spike in commits at the start of a project, it would die out shortly.

### Recovery Triggers

Despite feature commits indicating large gaps, it was the very same commits which suggested an eventual comeback. Many large gaps were sandwiched by feature commits, suggesting that the gap was simply a result of diligent work on a single repository. Half of these commits were usually unreadable merge commits, suggesting that the work was performed on a fork.

### Recovery Signals Before Gaps

As stated in the previous section, having feature commits before a gap is a good indicator the project will recover. However, this slightly challenges the statement earlier that large spikes at the beginning of projects can be a sign of a project not recovering. Generally, the projects which recovered were projects which had already recovered in the past from gaps. These kinds of projects typically have work slowly being done over months, only for a commit after a gap has started.

### Interpretation Pitfalls

There were three common pitfalls:
1. I was a lot more strict in determining whether a project recovered after a gap. If only a handfull of maintenance commit batches had been made since the gap, I would have said the project died. In retrospect, I think this was too harsh. Projects often meet their deliverable and enter a maintenance phase; I would argue now that these projects are still alive and well. In these cases, me and my partner would review the graph of commits and reevaluate.
2. Tieing to the previous point, I was also strict as to whether a project was still active. I think I was being too pessimistic, saying that projects were dead--even if they weren't. In these cases, me and my partner would review the graph of commits and reevaluate.
3. There were times when either me or my partner would misinterpret one or two commits when determining recent themes. A quick discussion was enough to fix these sorts of disagreements, however.
